Teaching & mentoring

Learning to read
the landscape.

My goal is to help students turn observations into questions and develop explanations they can defend. A terrace above a river, a pattern in a terrain map, or an unexpected model result can begin the same learning process: describe the evidence, propose an explanation, and decide what would test it.

Begin with observations

I ask students to distinguish what they see from what they infer. Sketches, predictions, and comparisons between plausible explanations make reasoning visible and give students a way to revise their ideas.

Build quantitative confidence in stages

Worked examples lead into guided analysis and then independent investigation. Students can enter a problem with different strengths, from careful field observations to coding experience. Clear criteria and opportunities to revise help distinguish scientific reasoning from the polish of an output.

Make room for unfinished ideas

Brief partner discussions give students time to develop an explanation before presenting it. Rotating group responsibilities lets them practice unfamiliar skills, while questions about evidence turn disagreement into a useful part of scientific work.

Plan access into the learning activity

For field learning, I explain objectives and physical demands, share maps and materials in advance, and plan equivalent ways to meet an objective when a location or task is inaccessible. Private check-ins give students another way to raise concerns.

Teaching in practice

Testing tectonic forcing in a model

Earth Surface Processes · Indiana University

I developed a modeling assignment in which students varied tectonic forcing and examined the landscape response. Field examples helped connect the simulated patterns to physical interpretations.

Choosing the next field observation

Geology Summer Field Camp · Colorado State University

In a field-camp module on mapping surface deposits and interpreting past floods, I used questions to help students decide what to observe next. The aim was to give enough guidance for progress while leaving the interpretation in their hands.

Research mentoring

From guided participation to scientific ownership

I have mentored undergraduate and graduate researchers in field sampling, laboratory preparation and analysis, digital topography, computational workflows, and numerical modeling. Mentees have contributed to abstracts and presentations at AGU and GSA meetings.

My approach gives students increasing responsibility for the scientific choices within a project. A discussion of a figure becomes a discussion of which result they trust, what evidence could change the interpretation, and which analysis should come next. As confidence grows, students take ownership of datasets, explanations, and the direction of research conversations.
